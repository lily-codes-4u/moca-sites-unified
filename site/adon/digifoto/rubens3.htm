Don frequently haunts museums and galleries with his trusty digital camera.

A MEMBER OF THE FOURMENT FAMILY

Peter Paul Rubens. Flemish. 1577-1640. Oil on wood. Metropolitan Museum of Art, NYC.

Rubens was one of the great European masters and an institution unto himself. Born of a profligate lawyer father who spent time in jail for adultery (and who was finally extricated upon the efforts of his wife), Rubens travelled and studied widely in Italy and Spain as a young man, and returned to Antwerp where he was a favorite of the court both as man and artist. His workshop attracted the the most promising students of the day, as Rubens and his apprentices re-made the face of northern European art in the ensuing years. His scale was huge, his energy unflagging, and his talent as colorist and dramatist prodigious. He had gout, his career was long and successful, and he grew rich. His work adorned (and still adorns, in most cases) churches, palaces and public buildings through much of Europe. He served as diplomat on sensitive missions for his country. He was a good Catholic in the era of the counter-reformation and his religion as expressed in his art proved good propaganda for the royalist families of the age, who supported him enthusiastically. His themes derived from the Bible and from mythology. Widowed in his fifties, he married 15-year old Helen Fourment and had a second family. On the evidence, she was a beauty, a condition his first wife could not aspire to. The portrait shown here is that of a member of his second wife's family.

Rubens' work has majesty but is just a little bit too grand and glorious for my own modern taste. His women and children are just too rosy-cheeked and exquisite, and the dramatics of his large works a little too self-conscious. Artists should suffer. Except for his gout, Rubens did not quite suffer enough. His career overlapped with that of Rembrandt, who lived in another city and country and with another temperament.
